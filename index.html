01

Review · The economic problem

Scarcity means not every want can be met at the same time

Keep one chain in mind: limited resources + unlimited wants → choices must be made → the next-best alternative is given up → opportunity cost arises. A PPF shows this chain.

Limited resourcesLand, labour, capital and enterprise are finite
Unlimited wantsPeople want more goods and services than can be produced
ChoiceResources must be allocated between competing uses
Opportunity costEach choice gives up the next-best alternative

Scarcity

AQA definition

“The fundamental economic problem that results from limited resources and unlimited wants. It means that choices have to be made which have an opportunity cost.”

fundamental economic problemEvery economy faces it, not only low-income countries.
limited resourcesFactors of production are finite; “limited” does not mean none exist.
unlimited wantsWants continue to exceed what existing resources can satisfy.
choices have to be madeThe same resource cannot serve every competing use at once, so a choice is necessary.
opportunity costThe next-best use is given up, creating the real cost of the choice.

Choice

Teacher formulation

The act of selecting one option from competing alternatives because resources cannot satisfy them all.

selecting one optionResources are actually committed to one use; this is more than listing preferences.
competing alternativesTwo uses compete for the same scarce resource; otherwise there is no economic trade-off.

Opportunity cost

AQA definition

“The next best alternative foregone when a choice is made.”

next bestUse only the second-best option, not the sum of everything rejected.
alternativeIt must be a genuine use that could have been chosen.
foregoneIt is lost because the current option was chosen; both cannot be obtained.
choice is madeOpportunity cost arises from choice and is not another name for the money price.

Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)

AQA definition

“It shows the various quantities of two goods or services that can be produced, with the current state of technology, when all the available resources are fully employed.”

various quantitiesEach point on the frontier is a different combination of output, not a single answer.
two goods or servicesThe two-dimensional model simplifies a complex economy into two types of output.
can be producedIt describes current productive capacity and therefore which combinations are feasible.
current technologyTechnology is assumed to remain unchanged when moving along one PPF.
available resourcesThe quantity and quality of land, labour, capital and enterprise are given.
fully employedPoints on the frontier use resources fully and efficiently; points inside show unemployment or inefficiency.

Real example: NHS operating-theatre time

An operating theatre has room for only one main use on Friday afternoon. If four cataract operations are chosen and the next-best option is one heart operation, the opportunity cost is the one heart operation foregone. It is not the theatre rent or the total of every other possible operation.

Definition source: AQA, A-level Economics 7136 Subject Specific Vocabulary (2024). “Choice” is a teacher formulation used here to connect the AQA concepts.
02

Review · Specialisation and exchange

Specialisation raises output, while exchange shares the gains

Specialisation means concentrating resources on a narrower range of tasks. It usually raises productivity, but it also makes workers, firms and countries more interdependent.

Specialisation

AQA definition

“Where firms, regions, countries or factors of production concentrate on producing a particular good or service, or carrying out a particular task.”

firms, regions, countries or factorsSpecialisation can occur at the level of a worker, firm, region or country.
concentrateResources focus on a narrower range instead of being spread evenly across every task.
particular good or serviceFor example, a country may concentrate on financial services or semiconductors.
particular taskUnder division of labour, a worker completes only one part of the production process.

Money

AQA definition

“Primarily a medium of exchange or means of payment, but also a store of value.”

medium of exchangeBuyers exchange generally accepted money for goods, avoiding the double coincidence of wants required by barter.
means of paymentMoney settles transactions and debts, allowing exchange to take place more flexibly over time.
store of valuePurchasing power can be carried into the future, although high inflation weakens this function.
Repeated taskPractice improves skill in a narrow task
Less switchingLess time is lost changing tasks and tools
Capital matchedSpecialised machinery can fit a particular task
Higher productivityThe same time and resources produce more output

Why does money make exchange easier?

Problem with barterHow money solves it
You want bread, but the baker may not want your apples.Sell the apples for money and use the money to buy bread; the two wants no longer need to match.
A bicycle cannot easily be divided into exactly 400 units of bread.A common unit of account makes the prices of different goods comparable.
Apples rot, so they store purchasing power poorly.Relatively stable money can carry purchasing power into the future.

Specialisation also has costs

Benefits: greater skill, less switching time, easier mechanisation and larger scale.
Costs: repetitive work, narrower skills and more interdependent supply chains; disruption in one stage can affect other producers.

In an exam, explain the mechanism instead of listing points: “repetition → skill improves → output per worker per hour rises”.

Definition source: AQA Subject Specific Vocabulary (2024), “Specialisation” and “Money”.
03

Review · Methodology of economics

Separate testable claims from judgements about what ought to happen

Economists use observations to frame questions, assumptions to isolate relationships and evidence to test explanations. The difference between positive and normative statements is not whether a claim is correct, but whether it contains a value judgement.

Positive statement

AQA definition

“A statement that does not include a value judgement and can be tested against the facts or evidence.”

does not include value judgementIt does not depend on moral claims such as good, bad, fair or ought.
can be testedEvidence can support or reject it in principle; the statement can still be false.
facts or evidenceData, natural experiments, surveys and observations can all be used to test it.

Normative statement

AQA definition

“A statement that includes a value judgement and cannot be refuted just by looking at data or evidence.”

includes value judgementIt states what is better, fairer or ought to happen.
cannot be refuted just by dataData can show consequences, but cannot decide whether society should value equity, freedom or efficiency most.

Assumption

Teacher formulation

A simplifying condition held to be true so economists can isolate a relationship and build a model.

simplifying conditionSome real-world complexity is temporarily held constant; this does not claim reality always works that way.
isolate a relationshipFor example, ceteris paribus holds other determinants of demand constant to study a price change.

Observation

Teacher formulation

A recorded pattern or event in the real world that provides evidence for forming or testing an economic explanation.

recorded pattern or eventIt must be describable or measurable rather than a vague impression.
evidenceAn observation may fit a theory, but a single before-and-after change rarely proves causation.
ObserveIdentify a price or behaviour pattern
HypothesisePropose a testable mechanism
AssumeHold other important factors constant
TestCompare the prediction with evidence

Quick check

Positive: “The average sugar content of drinks covered by the levy fell after it was introduced.” This can be tested with data.
Normative: “The government should increase the sugar levy.” The word “should” introduces a value judgement.

Limits of evidence: correlation does not automatically prove causation

The UK government reported a 46% fall in the average sugar content of drinks covered by the levy between 2015 and 2020. This is observable positive evidence. A possible mechanism is “higher sugar content → larger tax liability → stronger reward for reformulation”. To claim that the levy caused the whole 46% fall, however, we also need a credible counterfactual: what would have happened without the policy.

Official evidence: HM Treasury / HMRC, Strengthening the Soft Drinks Industry Levy consultation, Executive summary.

04

Review · Markets, demand and supply

A market is an arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together

A price change causes movement along a curve; a change in any other determinant shifts the whole curve. At equilibrium, the two quantities are equal and price has no tendency to change.

Market

Teacher formulation

An arrangement through which buyers and sellers interact to exchange a good, service or factor of production and determine price and quantity.

arrangementIt may be a physical shop, an online platform or a labour market; it need not have a fixed location.
buyers and sellers interactDemanders and suppliers interact through offers, choices and transactions.
determine price and quantityThe interaction produces a market price and quantity traded; demand alone does not determine them.

Demand curve

AQA definition

“The relationship between price and quantity demanded of a good or service, in a given period of time, when other things that affect demand are held constant.”

price and quantity demandedThey are normally inversely related; a price change causes movement along the demand curve.
given period of timeQuantity demanded needs a time period, such as 2,000 cups per week.
other things held constantIncome, preferences and the prices of substitutes are fixed: ceteris paribus.

Supply curve

AQA definition

“The relationship between price and quantity supplied of a good or service, in a given period of time, when other things that affect supply are held constant.”

price and quantity suppliedThey are normally positively related; a higher price increases the reward from extra production.
given period of timeQuantity supplied must also refer to a clear time period.
other things held constantChanges in costs, technology, taxes or weather shift the whole supply curve.

Equilibrium market price

AQA definition

“The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied and there is no tendency for the price to change.”

Qd = QsThe planned quantity bought equals the planned quantity sold; both sides determine equilibrium.
no tendency to changeThere is no excess demand to raise price and no excess supply to lower it.

How are markets linked? First identify whether the link works through demand or supply

Competitive demand

AQA definition

Demand for one product rises while demand for the other decreases: the goods are substitutes.

substitutesA rise in the price of butter may increase demand for margarine; XED is normally positive.

Joint demand

AQA definition

Two products are demanded together; demand for one is directly related to demand for the other.

demanded togetherA fall in printer prices may increase demand for ink cartridges; complements normally have negative XED.

Derived demand

AQA definition

Demand for a product or factor is determined by demand for a different product.

determined by another demandGreater demand for electric vehicles raises demand for battery engineers and lithium.

Composite demand

AQA definition

A product has more than one use; more demand for one use leaves less supply available elsewhere.

one input, several usesUsing more milk for cheese can leave less milk available for butter.

Joint supply

AQA definition

Output of one product also results in the output of a different product.

one process, several outputsRaising more sheep increases both mutton output and wool supply.

Writing a chain of analysis

Start with the change in the first market, then state the relationship between the goods, and finish with the curve, price and quantity in the second market. Do not jump straight to the conclusion.

AQA definition source: Demand curve, Supply curve, Equilibrium market price, Joint demand, Competitive demand, Composite demand, Derived demand and Joint supply, AQA Subject Specific Vocabulary (2024). “Market” is a teacher formulation.

Review · Elasticities

Elasticity measures how responsive one variable is to another

The denominator is own price for PED, income for YED and another product’s price for XED. Write the formula first, interpret the magnitude or sign, and then explain the economic relationship.

Price elasticity of demand (PED)

AQA definition

A measure of the extent to which quantity demanded changes in response to a change in the product’s own price.

extentIt measures the proportionate response, not only its direction.
quantity demandedThe numerator is the percentage change in quantity demanded.
own priceThe denominator is the percentage change in the price of the same product.

Income elasticity of demand (YED)

AQA definition

A measure of the extent to which quantity demanded changes in response to a change in income.

incomeThe denominator is a change in consumer income, not the product’s price.
sign mattersA positive value indicates a normal good; a negative value indicates an inferior good.

Cross elasticity of demand (XED)

AQA definition

A measure of the extent to which demand for one product changes in response to a change in the price of a different product.

different productThe numerator is quantity demanded of product A; the denominator is the price of product B.
sign shows relationshipA positive value indicates substitutes, a negative value indicates complements, and a value near zero indicates a weak relationship.
PED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP
Usually negative; classifications normally use |PED|
YED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔY
The sign distinguishes normal from inferior goods
XEDA,B = %ΔQdA ÷ %ΔPB
The sign distinguishes substitutes from complements

Interpreting the result

ResultInterpretationMeaning
PED = −0.4|PED| < 1, price inelasticA 1% price change causes a less than 1% change in Qd in the opposite direction
PED = −1.8|PED| > 1, price elasticThe percentage change in Qd is greater than the percentage price change
YED = +1.6Normal and income elasticDemand grows faster than income, as is common for a luxury
YED = −0.3Inferior goodDemand falls as income rises
XED = +2.0Relatively strong substitutesA rise in the price of B causes a marked increase in demand for A
XED = −0.7ComplementsA rise in the price of B reduces demand for A

PED and total revenue

Elastic demand: a price fall causes a larger percentage rise in Qd, so TR rises.
Inelastic demand: a price rise causes a smaller percentage fall in Qd, so TR rises.

TR = P × Q. Compare the percentage changes in P and Q rather than memorising the conclusion alone.

Calculation trap

A percentage change must be divided by the original value. If price rises from £0.80 to £0.84, the change is 0.04/0.80 = 5%, not 4%. A rise in quantity from 20,000 to 32,500 is 62.5%. Therefore, XED = 62.5%/5% = +12.5.

Definition source: AQA Subject Specific Vocabulary (2024), Price elasticity of demand, Income elasticity of demand and Cross elasticity of demand.

Review · Welfare, tax and market failure

Market prices create surplus; taxes redistribute it; market failure misallocates resources

Producer surplus is not profit. An indirect tax raises production costs and shifts supply left. The government receives revenue from transactions that still take place, while the lost gains from trades that disappear form deadweight loss.

Producer surplus (PS)

AQA definition

“The difference between the price a firm actually receives and the price it would be willing and able to supply the product for.”

differenceCompare the actual price with the minimum acceptable price for each unit, then add the amounts.
actually receivesIn a competitive market, this is normally the market equilibrium price.
willing and able to supplyThe supply curve shows the minimum price required to produce each additional unit.

Indirect tax

AQA definition

“A tax levied on spending. The burden of an indirect tax can be passed on to someone else, for example by raising the price of the product.”

levied on spendingIt is charged when a good or service is bought, for example VAT or a specific tax per unit.
can be passed onA legal duty on firms to pay the tax does not mean firms bear the whole economic burden.
raising the priceFirms may pass some of the burden to consumers; the extent depends on PED and PES.
Government revenue = tax per unit × quantity sold after tax

Relative elasticity determines tax incidence

The more price inelastic demand is, the harder it is for consumers to avoid the tax by buying less, so consumers usually bear a larger share. When demand is more elastic, firms find it harder to raise price and producers bear more of the burden.

PS ≠ profit

Producer surplus is the area by which market price exceeds the minimum supply price. Profit also deducts fixed costs, so the two terms are not interchangeable.

Market failure

AQA definition

“When markets operating without government intervention result in a misallocation of resources.”

without government interventionBegin with the free-market outcome, then judge whether it differs from the social optimum.
misallocation of resourcesToo much, too little or none of the output society needs is produced.

Merit good

AQA definition

“A product that society judges to be especially worthwhile and is likely to be underconsumed because the benefits are not always fully appreciated.”

society judges worthwhileThe definition contains a value judgement; education and vaccines are common examples, depending on context.
underconsumedFree-market consumption is below the level society considers desirable.
benefits not fully appreciatedMissing information, short-term thinking or cognitive bias leads consumers to undervalue future or external benefits.
Benefit underestimatedConsumers focus only on the immediate cost
Private demand too lowWillingness to pay is lower than with full information
Market Q too lowUnderconsumption occurs in the free market
InterventionSubsidies, information or direct provision can raise consumption

Example: vaccination

A person may underestimate the future private benefit of avoiding illness and may overlook the external benefit of reducing transmission to others. Market demand and consumption may therefore fall below the social optimum. Information campaigns can improve awareness, subsidies can lower the consumer price, and direct government provision can also address ability to pay. The effectiveness of each policy still depends on its cost, implementation and the risk of government failure.

Definition source: AQA Subject Specific Vocabulary (2024), Producer surplus, Indirect tax, Market failure and Merit good.

Rational economic decision making

AQA definition

Using all available information to select the best option to maximise the welfare of the decision maker.

all available informationThe decision maker gathers and correctly processes the relevant costs and benefits.
best optionThe option is not merely acceptable; it is the best after comparison.
maximise welfareA consumer seeks the greatest satisfaction within a budget constraint.

Marginal utility (MU)

AQA definition

The change in total utility from consuming one more or one fewer unit.

change in total utilityMU is the change in total satisfaction, not total satisfaction itself.
one more / fewer“Marginal” always focuses on the change from the next unit.

Imperfect information

AQA definition

An agent does not have all information needed for a rational decision, or information is distorted.

missing or distortedEither party may lack information about quality, risk or long-term cost.

Asymmetric information

AQA definition

A type of imperfect information where one party has more information than the other.

one party knows moreA used-car seller normally knows more about the vehicle’s faults than the buyer.

Nudge

AQA definition

Something that encourages a decision or behaviour without removing freedom of choice.

encouragesDefaults, order or salience are changed to make one choice easier.
freedom remainsOther options remain available; a ban is not a nudge.

Key points from the official mark scheme

1. Scarcity and maximum outputThe boundary shows maximum combinations attainable with current resources and technology. A point outside is currently unattainable.
2. Choice and opportunity costMoving along the boundary reallocates resources. More of one output requires less of the other; the foregone output is the opportunity cost.
3. Efficiency and growthA point inside shows unemployment or inefficient use. Moving to the boundary uses existing capacity; an outward shift shows increased productive capacity.

Strong analytical chain: finite resources prevent every want from being met → the economy must choose a point on or within the boundary → moving towards one output sacrifices some of the other → the slope or labelled change measures opportunity cost. Specialised resources can make this cost increase.

The price of a packet of peanuts rises from £0.80 to £0.84. The number of packets of cashew nuts purchased per month rises from 20,000 to 32,500, while the price of cashew nuts is unchanged. The cross elasticity of demand for cashew nuts with respect to the price of peanuts is
